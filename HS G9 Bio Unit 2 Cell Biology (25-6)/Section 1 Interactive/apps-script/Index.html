B · Reading

Three big ideas

B1. What makes something alive?

Biologists agree on seven characteristics that, taken together, separate the living from the not-living. None of them on their own is enough. A candle uses energy. A crystal grows. A river responds to gravity. But only a living thing checks every box.

1. Living things are made of one or more cells. This is the smallest physical unit that does the work of life. A bacterium is a single cell. A blue whale is roughly 100 quadrillion of them. The microbes in idli batter — the yeast Saccharomyces and the lactic acid bacteria Leuconostoc and Lactobacillus — are all cells. The batter itself is not. The batter is their habitat.

2. Living things show order. The atoms inside a living thing aren't arranged randomly. They are organized into specific molecules, those molecules into structures, those structures into cells. If you scrambled the parts of a yeast cell on a benchtop, you would have all the same atoms — and no yeast.

3. Living things use energy. Every cell takes in molecules and breaks them apart to release energy it can use. Inside the idli batter, yeast cells grab sugars from the soaked rice and dal, break them down, and release energy that powers their own growth and reproduction. The carbon dioxide gas that bubbles out is the leftover. The bubbles are what make the batter rise.

Batter check

The batter rising is not just a chemistry trick. It is the visible signature of yeast cells eating. The volume increase is the gas the yeast cannot use, escaping into the dough.

4. Living things grow and develop. A cell does not just get larger over time — it changes in predictable ways. A yeast cell grows to a certain size, then divides. In a multi-cellular organism, a single fertilized cell can become a kidney, a leaf, a wing.

5. Living things reproduce. Every cell came from a previous cell. The yeasts in your grandmother's batter were not built from sugar and water overnight. They were already there — probably brought in on the rice, the dal, the bowl, or the air — and they divided. A single yeast cell can split every 20 to 30 minutes in warm batter. After eight hours, one cell can become more than a million.

6. Living things respond to their environment. Idli batter ferments faster in Chennai's heat than it does in a cool kitchen in the hills. The microbes notice the temperature. They notice when sugars run out. They notice when the batter gets too acidic to keep working in. The response is built into the chemistry of the cell.

7. Living things maintain homeostasis. "Homeostasis" means keeping your internal conditions stable even when the outside changes. The lactic acid bacteria in idli batter pump out acid as a waste product — but they also pump protons out of their own cells to keep their insides from becoming too acidic to function. Your body does the same kind of thing constantly: temperature, blood sugar, water balance, all held within narrow ranges.

None of these traits on its own proves a thing is alive. A flame uses energy. A salt crystal grows. A thermostat responds to its environment. The test is the full set, together, in one system. By that test, the microbes in idli batter pass clearly. The batter itself — flour, water, microbes — does not. The batter is the habitat. The cells are the life.

B2. Where cell theory came from

Cells were not always obvious. For most of human history, people had no reason to think bodies were made of microscopic units. The idea of a cell required a tool that did not exist until the 1600s: the microscope. The story of cell theory is partly a story of the lenses.

Robert Hooke, 1665. An English scientist working at the Royal Society, Hooke built a compound microscope — two lenses, one above the other — and used it to look at thin slices of cork. What he saw were rows of empty rectangular boxes, like the small rooms in a monastery. He called them "cells," from the Latin cellae. He wasn't looking at living material. He was looking at the dead cell walls of a tree. But the name stuck.

Anton van Leeuwenhoek, 1670s and onward. A Dutch draper with no formal scientific training, Leeuwenhoek ground his own single-lens microscopes. They were small — about the size of a thumb — but they could magnify up to 270 times, far better than Hooke's instruments. With them, Leeuwenhoek was the first person to see living single-celled organisms. He called them "animalcules" — little animals — and described them in letters to the Royal Society. Pond water was full of them. So was scraped tooth plaque. Life, it turned out, was everywhere.

Schleiden and Schwann, 1838–1839. Almost two centuries after Hooke, two German scientists made the leap from "cells exist" to "cells are universal." Matthias Schleiden, working with plants, concluded in 1838 that all plant tissues are made of cells. The next year, Theodor Schwann extended the claim to animals. Together, they had the first two principles of cell theory: all living things are made of cells, and the cell is the basic unit of structure and function. They were wrong about one thing — they thought cells might form spontaneously from a kind of formless "cell-forming substance." That part needed correction.

Rudolf Virchow, 1855. The correction came from a German physician. Virchow drew on the work of others to formalize the third principle: omnis cellula e cellula — every cell from a cell. Cells do not appear from nothing. They reproduce. This closed the book on spontaneous generation as an explanation for life, and gave cell theory its modern form:

Modern cell theory

  1. All living things are made of one or more cells.
  2. The cell is the basic unit of structure and function in living things.
  3. All cells come from pre-existing cells.

The third principle, applied to the bowl of batter on the counter: the yeasts and bacteria multiplying overnight did not come from nothing. They were already in the rice, the dal, the bowl, the air. Their ancestors were yeasts and bacteria. So were their ancestors' ancestors. The chain goes back, unbroken, to the first cells on Earth.

B3. Levels of organization

One of the strangest moves in biology is the jump from "not alive" to "alive." Atoms aren't alive. Molecules aren't alive. Somewhere between molecules and cells, life appears. The eight levels of biological organization name this climb from small to large, from non-living parts to living wholes, and then from single living units to coordinated systems.

Use a rice plant as the example, the same crop that becomes the rice in idli batter.

  1. Atom. The smallest unit of matter that keeps the properties of an element. A single carbon atom in a chlorophyll molecule inside a rice leaf.
  2. Molecule. Two or more atoms bonded together. The chlorophyll molecule itself — about a hundred atoms — absorbs sunlight.
  3. Organelle. A specialized structure inside a cell, doing one job. Chloroplasts hold the chlorophyll and run photosynthesis.
  4. Cell. The first level that is alive. A single mesophyll cell in a rice leaf is packed with chloroplasts and does photosynthesis as a complete unit. This is where the seven characteristics of life first show up together.
  5. Tissue. Many similar cells working together to do one job. A layer of mesophyll cells inside the leaf is a tissue: every cell does photosynthesis, and together they form a sheet that catches light efficiently.
  6. Organ. Several tissues working together to do a larger job. A rice leaf is an organ — mesophyll, veins, and epidermis tissues — that converts sunlight into sugar.
  7. Organ system. A group of organs working together to do a major job. The shoot system of a rice plant — leaves, stem, tiller — together feed the whole organism.
  8. Organism. The complete living thing. One rice plant in a paddy, with its shoot system, root system, and reproductive system working in parallel.

Two of these jumps deserve attention. The jump from molecule (level 2) to cell (level 4) is the place where chemistry becomes biology — non-living parts arranged in the right way produce something that does all seven characteristics of life. The jump from cell (level 4) to tissue (level 5) is the move from one specialist to a team of specialists. Both jumps are about new properties appearing because of how the parts are organized, not because of any single new ingredient.
