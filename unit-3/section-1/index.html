Where cell theory came from

Cells were not always obvious. For most of human history, nobody had any reason to think a body was built out of microscopic units. The idea of a cell needed a tool that did not exist until the 1600s. So the story of cell theory is partly a story of lenses: each step happened when an instrument got good enough to show something new, and the claim could never run ahead of what the instrument could show.

Fill your evidence table as you read. Four columns: scientist, year, what they saw, what it proved.

Robert Hooke, 1665. An English scientist at the Royal Society, Hooke built a compound microscope, two lenses one above the other, and used it on thin slices of cork. What he saw was rows of empty rectangular boxes, like the small rooms in a monastery. He called them cells, from the Latin cellae. He was not looking at living material at all. He was looking at the dead cell walls of a tree, and his instrument reached about thirty times, so nothing inside them could have moved for him. The name stuck anyway.

Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, 1670s onward. A Dutch draper with no scientific training, Leeuwenhoek ground his own lenses, one lens per instrument, each about the size of a thumb. They magnified up to about two hundred and forty times, far past anything Hooke had. With them he became the first person to see living single-celled organisms. He called them animalcules, little animals, and wrote to the Royal Society about them. Pond water was full of them. So was the plaque he scraped off his own teeth. This is the step that turned a cell from a shape into something that could be alive, and it happened because of a lens.

Schleiden and Schwann, 1838 to 1839. Almost two centuries after Hooke, two German scientists made the jump from "cells exist" to "cells are everywhere." Matthias Schleiden, working on plants, concluded in 1838 that all plant tissue is made of cells. The next year Theodor Schwann said the same of animals. Neither could have claimed all living things on his own, which is why the two names are always said together. Between them they had the first two principles: living things are made of cells, and the cell is the basic unit of structure and function. They were wrong about one thing. They thought new cells might form out of a kind of shapeless "cell-forming substance," which is spontaneous generation wearing a lab coat.

Rudolf Virchow, 1855. A German physician who watched cells divide, and who pulled the observations of several people together into the third principle: omnis cellula e cellula, every cell from a cell. Cells do not appear. They reproduce.

Louis Pasteur, 1859. You already know this one from Unit 1. Pasteur boiled broth in a flask whose neck he drew out into a long S-bend. Air could reach the broth the whole time; dust could not, because it settled in the bend. The broth stayed clear for months. Tip the flask so the broth washes into the bend and back, and it clouds within days. The dust was carrying the cells. Virchow had said cells come from cells; Pasteur is the experiment that made the alternative impossible to keep believing.

Modern cell theory

  1. All living things are made of one or more cells.
  2. The cell is the basic unit of structure and function in living things.
  3. All cells come from pre-existing cells.

Every one of those three is a claim somebody had to see evidence for, which is why Checkpoint 1 asks you for the scientist and the instrument, not just the name.

Batter check

Apply the third principle to the bowl on the counter. The yeasts and bacteria that multiplied overnight did not come from nothing. They were already on the rice, the dal, the bowl, the air. Their parents were yeasts and bacteria, and so were theirs. The chain runs back unbroken to the first cells on Earth.
