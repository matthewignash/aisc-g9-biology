B · Reading

Surface, volume, and what a growing cell does about it

B1. Everything goes in and out through the surface

Everything a cell needs comes in through its surface: sugar, oxygen, water. Everything it throws out leaves the same way. But the cell does not use those things at the surface. It uses them throughout its whole volume, in every part of the cytoplasm, in every mitochondrion, all the way to the middle.

So a cell has two numbers that matter, and they are in competition. Its surface area is how much door it has. Its volume is how much inside there is to serve. What decides whether a cell can keep up is not either number on its own. It is the ratio between them: how much surface there is for every unit of volume.

Take a cube with a side of 1 cm. Surface area is 6 × 1 × 1, which is 6 cm². Volume is 1 × 1 × 1, which is 1 cm³. Six units of surface for every unit of volume, so the ratio is 6 : 1.

Now double the side to 2 cm. Surface area is 6 × 2 × 2, which is 24 cm². Volume is 2 × 2 × 2, which is 8 cm³. Both numbers went up, and the surface went up a lot. But the volume went up more. Three units of surface per unit of volume now. The ratio halved.

Look at the 10 cm row. The volume has overtaken the surface area outright. There is now less than one unit of door for every unit of inside that needs feeding.

Here is the reason, and it is the whole idea in one line. Surface area grows with the square of the side. Volume grows with the cube of it. A cube always wins against a square in the end. That is not a fact about cells. It is a fact about shapes, and cells are stuck with it.

B2. What a growing cell does about it

A cell that keeps growing runs into a hard limit. Past a certain size, the middle of the cell is waiting too long for the food and oxygen that have to travel in from the surface, and sitting too long in the waste that has to travel back out. Nothing breaks. It just gets slower and slower, until the middle of the cell is not being served.

So what does a cell do when its ratio has fallen too far? It divides.

This is worth being precise about, because it is easy to get backwards. Dividing does not change how much cell there is. Two cells of half the volume each hold, between them, exactly the volume the one cell held. What changes is the surface. Cutting one cell into two makes new outside where there used to be inside, so the total surface area goes up while the total volume stays the same. Exchange speeds up again, because every unit of inside now has more door.

Back to the bowl

This is why the batter doubled and the cells did not. The bowl did not grow because its yeast cells got bigger. It grew because there came to be more of them. Every one of those cells hit the same limit, divided, and handed the problem to two smaller cells that could cope with it. That is the third principle of cell theory doing an actual job.

You will not learn the stages of cell division in this course. Grade 7 covered them, and they come back later. What you need is the reason: a cell divides because its ratio fell.

B3. An agar cube is not a cell

At Lab 2 you dropped agar cubes into acid and timed how long each one took to change color all the way to the center. That is a model, and every model is wrong somewhere. The agar has no membrane, so nothing about what enters it is selective: the acid goes wherever it can reach. It is not alive, it is not using what comes in, and the acid is not food. It does not grow, and it certainly does not divide. If you tried to explain a real cell using only the cube, you would get all of that wrong.

The cube gets exactly one thing right, and it is the one thing being tested: material enters through the surface and then has to travel to the center, and the bigger the block, the further that journey is. That is enough to make it a good model of the size problem and a bad model of everything else. Knowing which is which is a large part of what the summative asks you for.
