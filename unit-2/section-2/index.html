A · Phenomenon

Two kinds of life, sharing one bowl

Take a single drop of your fermenting idli batter and put it under a microscope. You don't see one kind of life. You see two. Bigger, rounder cells with visible internal structures — these are yeasts. Saccharomyces cerevisiae or a relative. They have a nucleus, mitochondria, a defined cytoskeleton. They are eukaryotic cells. In between the yeasts, you see much smaller shapes — rods, sometimes pairs, sometimes chains. These are lactic acid bacteria — Leuconostoc and Lactobacillus species. They have a cell membrane and DNA, but no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles. They are prokaryotic cells. Two kinds of life, sharing one bowl, eating the same sugars, producing different wastes.

B1 Two kinds of cells The defining difference: eukaryotes have a nucleus, prokaryotes don't.

Every cell biologists have ever found falls into one of two camps. Prokaryotic cells — bacteria and archaea — are small, simple, and ancient. Eukaryotic cells — animals, plants, fungi, and protists — are larger, more complex, and evolutionarily newer. The single biggest difference is the nucleus. Eukaryotes have one. Prokaryotes don't.

A nucleus is a membrane-wrapped compartment inside the cell that holds the DNA. Prokaryotes still have DNA — they couldn't be alive without it — but their DNA floats free in a region called the nucleoid. There is no membrane separating it from the rest of the cell. Prokaryotes also lack any membrane-bound organelles. No mitochondria, no Golgi, no endoplasmic reticulum. The cell is essentially one open room.

That open layout has trade-offs. It makes prokaryotes fast: a bacterial cell can divide in 20 minutes when conditions are right. And it makes them small — most are 0.5 to 5 micrometers across, compared to 10 to 100 micrometers for a typical eukaryotic cell. But the absence of compartments also limits what they can do. A eukaryotic cell can keep one chemistry over here, a different chemistry over there, and a destructive process safely sealed inside a third structure. A prokaryote has to do everything in the same room.

Both kinds of cell share some things, though. Both have a cell membrane — a thin phospholipid layer that separates inside from outside. Both have cytoplasm — the watery interior where reactions happen. Both have ribosomes — the molecular machines that build proteins. (Prokaryote ribosomes are slightly smaller — 70S — than the 80S ribosomes of eukaryotes, which is the basis for why some antibiotics can kill bacteria without poisoning you.) And most prokaryotes have a cell wall, made of a substance called peptidoglycan, on the outside of the membrane. Plants and fungi also have cell walls, but theirs are made of different materials — cellulose for plants, chitin for fungi.

The comparison table makes the differences look stark, but in a drop of idli batter you can see the partnership: prokaryotes and eukaryotes living together, eating the same sugars, producing different wastes, neither one replacing the other.

B2 Inside the eukaryotic cell — the organelles Fourteen organelles, what each one does, and why compartmentalization changed biology.

An "organelle" is a specialized structure inside a cell that does one job. Most are wrapped in their own membrane, which lets the cell run several chemistries at once without them interfering. This is the eukaryotic cell's biggest move: compartmentalization. Each compartment keeps its own conditions — its own pH, its own concentration of enzymes, its own building materials.

The major organelles, with what they do:

Nucleus

The cell's control center. A double membrane wraps the DNA — every instruction the cell will ever follow lives in here. Holes in the membrane (nuclear pores) let copies of those instructions move out to the rest of the cell.

Nucleolus

A dense region inside the nucleus where ribosomes are assembled. Not a separate organelle — more like a workshop room within the control center.

Ribosomes

The protein-building machinery. Some float free in the cytoplasm, making proteins for the cell to use itself. Others are bolted to the surface of the rough ER, making proteins that will get exported. Ribosomes are the smallest "machines" in the cell — small enough that even prokaryotes have them.

Rough endoplasmic reticulum (rough ER)

A folded sheet of membrane studded with ribosomes — that's what makes it "rough." Proteins built on these ribosomes get folded and threaded into the ER as they're made, ready for shipping.

Smooth endoplasmic reticulum (smooth ER)

The same kind of folded membrane, but without ribosomes. Smooth ER builds lipids, detoxifies harmful molecules in liver cells, and stores calcium in muscle cells.

Golgi apparatus

A stack of flattened membrane sacs that acts like the cell's mail room. Proteins arrive from the ER in vesicles, get sorted, modified (often by adding sugar groups), packaged into new vesicles, and sent to their destination — to other organelles, embedded in the cell membrane, or shipped out of the cell.

Mitochondria

The cell's power plants. Mitochondria run cellular respiration: they take in glucose and oxygen and release ATP, the molecule the rest of the cell uses to power its work. A muscle cell that's doing heavy work can have thousands of mitochondria. A skin cell has fewer. The yeasts in idli batter also have mitochondria — that's part of what makes them eukaryotic.

Chloroplasts (plants only)

Where photosynthesis happens. Chloroplasts capture sunlight and use it to build sugar from carbon dioxide and water. Only plants, algae, and a few protists have them. Plants still have mitochondria too — chloroplasts make the sugar, mitochondria release the energy. Both organelles are needed.

Large central vacuole (plant cells)

A big membrane-wrapped sac that takes up most of the volume of a mature plant cell. It stores water, sugars, salts, and waste products. When it's full, it pushes outward against the cell wall and gives the plant its rigid structure. When it's empty, leaves wilt.

Lysosomes (animal cells)

The cell's recycling crew. Lysosomes are membrane-wrapped sacs full of digestive enzymes — strong enough to break down old organelles, defective proteins, and engulfed bacteria. The membrane keeps those enzymes contained; if it broke, the cell would digest itself.

Cell membrane

The outer boundary of every cell. A double layer of phospholipids that decides what gets in and what stays out. Section 3 takes the membrane apart in detail.

Cell wall

A rigid outer layer found in plant cells, fungal cells, and most prokaryotes — but never animal cells. Plants use cellulose. Fungi use chitin. Bacteria use peptidoglycan. Same idea, different materials.

Cytoskeleton

A network of protein fibers — actin filaments, microtubules, intermediate filaments — that gives the cell its shape, moves materials around inside it, and helps it divide. Think of it as scaffolding plus a transport system.

Vesicles

Small membrane-bound sacs that shuttle materials between organelles. Vesicles bud off from one organelle, travel across the cytoplasm, and fuse with another. Most of the traffic inside a cell moves this way.

Why compartmentalize

Putting each job in its own room is the eukaryotic cell's superpower. Digestive enzymes can be active in a lysosome without dissolving the rest of the cell. ATP production can happen at full speed in a mitochondrion without overheating the cytoplasm. Different organelles can hold different pH values, different concentrations of enzymes, different building blocks — at the same time, in the same cell. Prokaryotes have to do everything in the same room. Eukaryotes get separate rooms for each task. That single architectural change is what lets eukaryotic cells be larger, more complex, and capable of multi-cellularity.

B3 Plant vs. animal cells Same kit, different edges — plus what the mushroom example tells you for the summative.

Every eukaryotic cell shares the same basic kit: nucleus, mitochondria, ribosomes, ER, Golgi, cytoplasm, cell membrane, cytoskeleton. The differences are at the edges of the diagram.

Plant cells have, on top of the standard eukaryotic kit, a cell wall made of cellulose, a large central vacuole, and chloroplasts. They generally lack centrioles and have only small vacuoles rather than the dedicated digestive lysosomes of animal cells.

Animal cells have lysosomes, centrioles, and small vacuoles. They don't have a cell wall, don't have chloroplasts, and don't have the big central vacuole. Their flexibility — being able to change shape, crawl, fuse — comes partly from not being boxed in by a rigid wall.

The third major group of eukaryotes — fungi — is worth knowing because it scrambles the pattern. A mushroom is a fungus. Fungi have cell walls (made of chitin, not cellulose), so they're walled like plants. But fungi don't have chloroplasts. They can't photosynthesize. So they get their food the way animals do — by absorbing nutrients from their surroundings. Fungi are heterotrophs: they take in food made by other organisms. A mushroom growing on a fallen log is digesting the wood from the outside, absorbing the simple sugars and amino acids back through its cell walls and membranes. Walled like a plant, fed like an animal.

B4 Where did the organelles come from? The endosymbiotic theory — and why you're partly descended from a swallowed bacterium.

Two billion years ago, the only cells on Earth were prokaryotes. There were no nuclei, no mitochondria, no chloroplasts — none of the inner machinery that defines a eukaryote. The fossil record shows the first cells with internal compartments appearing around that time. Where did those compartments come from?

The answer that biologists have converged on is called the endosymbiotic theory. ("Endo-" means inside; "symbiosis" means living together.) The idea is that some organelles inside eukaryotic cells were once independent prokaryotes that were swallowed by a larger ancestral cell. Instead of being digested, they survived inside and became permanent partners. Mitochondria were the swallowed cells that specialized in releasing energy from food. Chloroplasts were the swallowed cells that specialized in capturing sunlight. The host cell got an internal source of energy; the swallowed cell got a protected environment with steady nutrients. Over time, neither could live without the other.

The evidence is strong:

  • Mitochondria and chloroplasts have their own DNA. Not the same DNA as the rest of the cell — separate, circular DNA, more like a bacterium's. A muscle cell's mitochondria carry their own genome. A leaf cell's chloroplasts carry theirs.
  • They have their own ribosomes. And those ribosomes are 70S — bacterial style — not the 80S ribosomes the rest of the eukaryotic cell uses. This is why some antibiotics can disturb a person's mitochondria; the drug targets bacterial ribosomes, and the mitochondria still have them.
  • They have two membranes. An outer one and an inner one, consistent with one membrane from the host (when it engulfed the bacterium) and one from the bacterium itself.
  • They divide on their own schedule. Mitochondria and chloroplasts grow and split independently of the cell they live in — by a process more like binary fission (how bacteria divide) than the regular cell division of their host.

What this means, philosophically, is that every plant, every animal, every fungus — including you — is a chimera. Each of your cells is the descendant of an ancient cooperation between two different kinds of organism. The bacterial ancestors that became mitochondria are still inside you. They divide, they make their own proteins, they hand down their own DNA. Two billion years ago, two kinds of life living in the same place, eating the same sugars, became one. You are, in part, descended from a swallowed bacterium.

(And that closes the loop on the idli batter. Two kinds of life sharing one bowl, neither replacing the other. Two billion years ago, that partnership became permanent — and from it, eukaryotes and everything they built.)
